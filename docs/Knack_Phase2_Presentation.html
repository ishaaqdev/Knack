INTRODUCTION

  • Overview: Knack is an AI-powered web platform that develops essential soft skills through interactive, scenario-based learning, delivered as a five-minute daily habit.
  • Current Scenario: Engineering graduates often possess strong technical knowledge but lack "job-readiness" due to a deficit in soft skill training.
  • Relevance: Soft skills like communication and situational judgment are the most sought-after traits by global employers.
  • Status at Phase 2: The platform is fully implemented and running as a career simulation. The trainee is not asked questions about a workplace; they are placed inside one. They sit a group discussion, clear five interview rounds, join a company, work there through timed days made of messages, decisions and live conversations with colleagues who remember them, and finish with a performance review, a salary offer and a negotiation.
  • What is measured: ten professional attributes on a 0-100 scale that fall as well as rise, kept deliberately separate from XP. XP is what a trainee earns for turning up; an attribute is a claim about what they can actually do.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

The Core Problem: Over 60% of fresh graduates in India lack the soft skills required for the professional workforce.

Key Issues:

  • Educational Gap: Soft skills remain largely untaught in standard engineering curricula.
  • Passive Learning Failure: Existing solutions like workshops or MOOCs are passive and non-engaging, leading to low completion rates.
  • Measurement Difficulty: Traditional methods provide no credible way to measure or track situational judgment growth.
  • Cost of Personalisation: An AI tutor that remembers a learner normally grows more expensive with every session, which makes daily use unaffordable at scale.

OBJECTIVES

  • Bridge Readiness Gaps: Align academic learning with professional situational demands.
  • Dynamic Learning Environments: Deliver adaptive, real-world professional challenges generated for the individual learner.
  • Sustained Habit Formation: Turn soft-skill development into a daily ritual using XP, levels and streaks.
  • Measurable Progress Tracking: Visualise qualitative growth through per-track skill scores and a composite Professional Readiness Score.
  • Bounded Operating Cost: Keep the AI prompt a constant size no matter how long a learner has been training. (Added at implementation stage.)
  • Graceful Degradation: Remain usable when the free AI service is rate-limited. (Added at implementation stage.)
  • Assess Recognition and Production Separately: Test both the ability to choose the right move and the ability to write it. (Added at implementation stage.)
  • Rehearse the Real Gate: Give the learner a graded mock interview drawn from their own resume, since the placement interview is what the training is ultimately for. (Added at implementation stage.)
  • Teach Through Consequence, Not Correction: Place the learner inside a workplace where decisions affect relationships, opportunities and pay, and let those consequences arrive later than the decision that caused them. (Added at implementation stage.)

RESEARCH GAP

  • Passive vs. Active Learning: Existing platforms rely on passive video consumption, which lacks the active engagement necessary for behavioural change.
  • Lack of Real-Time Feedback: Curriculum-based training gives no immediate, AI-driven coaching on open-ended situational responses.
  • Stagnant Content: Most workshops use fixed scenarios that do not adapt to an individual student's performance or skill level.
  • Measurement Infrastructure: There is a significant gap in measuring and visualising soft-skill growth through quantitative metrics.
  • Unbounded Memory Cost: Systems that personalise from history typically inject past interactions into every prompt, so cost and latency grow without limit. No reviewed work addresses this for a daily-use educational tool.

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS — PERSONALISATION

Four independent signals are combined into every scenario. Each is bounded in size by construction, which is what keeps the prompt a constant length.

  • Stated profile (8 fields): academic year, branch, target role, prior team experience, preferred work setting, self-identified difficulties and confidence per track. Collected once at onboarding, editable later.
  • GitHub portfolio (optional): reads the learner's public repositories, discards forks and archived work, and reduces them to at most six technologies and three problem domains.
  • Resume (optional): the uploaded document is reduced to at most ten technologies, four projects and three factual claims. Only the technology list reaches a scenario prompt; the projects and claims are held for the interviewer, who is the one who should be asking about them.
  • Derived memory: a 60-word summary of what past attempts revealed, rewritten by the model every three attempts from a six-attempt window.
  • Scenario shape sampling: the counterpart, the medium, the moment and the pressure are chosen by the server from 18,480 combinations and passed to the model as requirements, rather than asking the model to vary its own output.

The prompt explicitly forbids restating the learner's profile in the scenario text — the background selects the setting and the stakes, and must never appear in the situation itself.

CHALLENGES FACED AND SOLUTIONS ADOPTED

ChallengeCause identifiedSolution adopted
Free AI quota exhausted within hours OpenRouter allows 50 free requests per day. Our retry policy used up to six requests per scenario. One attempt per model, rate limits never retried, and reset timestamps honoured through a cooldown
The fallback model failed identically Both models in the chain were served by the same upstream provider. Chain rebuilt across three independent providers
Every scenario read the same Instructing the model to vary its output changed the wording but not the structure. 18,480 scenario shapes sampled by the server and passed as requirements
Model fixated on one project The GitHub summary named a specific project and the model anchored on it. Summary kept for display; only the technology list is sent to the model
Profile read back in the scenario Scenarios opened "As a final-year CSE student aiming for…". Explicit rule: background sets the scene and must never appear in it

CHALLENGES — THE SIMULATION STAGE

ChallengeCause identifiedSolution adopted
Some models returned no output Nearly every free model now reasons before answering, and those hidden tokens are charged against the output ceiling. One spent 2,691 of them and returned nothing. Reasoning trace suppressed, effort lowered, and a fixed allowance added to every ceiling — a ceiling costs nothing when the model stops early
Feedback landed on the wrong answer The grader numbered its per-answer critiques from one while the code read them from zero, so every candidate saw criticism of a paragraph they had not written. Index base detected from the returned values; unanswered questions graded locally, never sent to the model
Attribute changes were silently discarded The ten attributes were seeded on the dashboard read, so a trainee who went from onboarding straight into a level had no attribute rows at all, and every delta written against them updated nothing. Seeding moved to the gameplay gate, where nothing can score before the thing it scores into exists. Found by an end-to-end run in which a 9/10 moved a relationship but no attributes
The review invented a colleague The end-of-internship review produced "Jordan has spoken up for you" on a team with no Jordan on it, despite the prompt forbidding it. Any sentence naming someone not on the team is dropped before display; a rule in a prompt is a request, and this one mattered too much to be one
A level took longer than anyone would wait Two model calls issued together queue behind each other on a free provider. The written stage is started when the level is served and collected while the trainee answers the four choices, so it is ready before they reach it
Port silently taken by another service The app worked only because the hostname resolved over IPv6. Moved to a free port and bound the loopback interface explicitly

FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS

  • Validate the grader against human raters. Collect expert scores for the same responses and measure agreement. This converts the present result — that the grader separates good answers from poor ones — into a claim that it is correct. Highest value next step.
  • Two-grader reconciliation. Sample two independent gradings and reconcile where they diverge, reducing variance at roughly double the inference cost.
  • Spoken interview rounds. The five written rounds already exist; adding audio capture and transcription would put them through the same grader in the medium a real interview actually uses.
  • Group discussion round. Several AI candidates with different personalities, scored on when the learner enters the discussion rather than how much they say — the one placement stage the current build does not rehearse.
  • Instructor dashboard. Anonymised, class-level weakness aggregation so a department can see which competencies to address in teaching.
  • Achievement badges, deferred from Phase 1 and compatible with the existing progression model.
  • Account recovery and a native mobile client. The interface is already responsive, so a mobile client is an incremental rather than architectural change.

CONCLUSION

  • Objectives met. A working platform delivers AI-generated, personalised workplace scenarios, grades free-text responses on a calibrated rubric, and tracks progress through XP, levels, streaks and a Professional Readiness Score.
  • Personalisation is demonstrable. Substituting the learner profile changes the scenario substantially; a linked GitHub account grounds scenarios in the technologies the learner actually uses; and an uploaded resume lets the mock interviewer ask about their projects by name.
  • Two skills are measured, not one. A level asks the learner to recognise the right move four times and then to write it once, and weights the writing higher — recognising good communication and producing it are not the same ability.
  • The simulation is the contribution. Ten attributes that fall as well as rise, colleagues who remember specific interactions, consequences that arrive two or three levels after the decision that caused them, and a salary negotiation with four possible outcomes. Together these produce something a quiz cannot: a trainee who accumulates XP while their Leadership score falls, and can see exactly why.
  • The principal contribution is bounded memory. By rewriting a short summary every three attempts rather than accumulating history, the system carries forward what it has learned at a constant cost. Its effect is visible in feedback that says "this is the same gap as last time" — a statement a stateless system cannot produce.
  • Limitations are acknowledged. The grader is unvalidated against human experts, testing used synthetic profiles, and no longitudinal study of learning outcomes has been carried out.

Appendix A: Workplace Communication

Focus: Navigating professional hierarchy and clarity.

  • The Rebuttal: You fundamentally disagree with your Team Lead's technical direction during a client briefing; deliver a professional rebuttal that protects project integrity without undermining authority.
  • The Credit Claim: A peer takes credit for your code or logic in a team meeting; reclaim your contribution professionally so your performance is accurately recorded.
  • The Crisis Update: A major deadline will be missed due to a server failure; draft a "no-excuse" update to your manager that proposes a recovery timeline.

Implemented: the system now generates scenarios of this kind automatically, varying the counterpart, the medium, the moment and the pressure on every generation.

Appendix B: Critical Thinking

Focus: Resource management and data-driven logic.

  • The Runway Decision: Your startup has exactly ₹50,000 remaining; justify spending it on urgent lead generation or on fixing a critical security bug.
  • The Presentation Pivot: During a project defence a panelist identifies a fundamental flaw in your logic; decide in real time whether to defend the architecture or pivot.
  • The Hiring Logic: Evaluate two candidates with identical CGPAs on situational judgment data and justify your selection against a competency rubric.

Implemented: difficulty for this track is set from the learner's rolling average of the last five scores, shifting one band at a time as the trend moves.

Appendix C: Digital & AI Fluency

Focus: Critical oversight of automated systems.

  • The Hallucination Audit: An AI-generated proposal contains two subtle factual errors about local regulations; identify them and evaluate the risk of sending the document.
  • The Component Design Challenge: Generate boilerplate for an analytics dashboard with an AI tool, identify a logic flaw in the state management, and refine the prompt so the design is scalable and secure.
  • The Ethical Risk Assessment: An organisation plans to automate all student counselling with language models; evaluate the top three ethical and privacy risks.

Implemented: this track grades whether the learner named a specific verification step, rather than whether they expressed general caution.
